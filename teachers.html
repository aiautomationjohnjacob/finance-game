What is FinanceGame?

FinanceGame is a free, browser-based financial literacy simulation for students in grades 8–12 and adult learners. Students play through monthly financial decisions — earning income, managing debt, investing, and responding to life events like job loss or medical bills. No accounts, no installs, no cost.

How to Use in the Classroom

  1. Individual exploration — Students open index.html on any device with a browser. Progress saves automatically to their device. Each session takes 10–20 minutes.
  2. Paired problem-solving — Two students per device; one decides, one explains their reasoning aloud. Switch roles each month.
  3. Whole-class debrief — Project the game on a screen. Make one decision together, then reveal consequences. Use discussion questions below.
  4. Teen classroom mode — Select "Jordan — High School" at setup for the age-appropriate starting scenario ($800/month, no debt, savings focus).

Key Features

  • Debt clock slider — Shows real-time interest cost as APR and payment amount change. Works on credit cards (24% APR default, reflecting 2025 CFPB data).
  • Amortization visualizer — Shows how little of a mortgage payment goes to principal in early years.
  • Simulated stock portfolio — Buy/sell fictional stocks; watch compound growth or loss.
  • Life event cards — Deck of 20 events (job loss, tax refund, medical bill, etc.) drive realistic decisions.
  • Month Summary — After each month, students see their cash flow delta and one-sentence decision quality feedback — no letter grades.

Technical Requirements

  • Any modern browser (Chrome, Firefox, Safari, Edge) — no plugins needed
  • Works offline once loaded; no internet required during play
  • Mobile-friendly (phones, tablets, Chromebooks all work)
  • Student progress stays on their device only — no data is sent anywhere

Recommended Session Structure (50-minute class)

  • 0–5 min — Open the game, create profile, choose scenario
  • 5–25 min — Play 3–5 game months independently
  • 25–40 min — Whole-class debrief using discussion questions
  • 40–50 min — Connect to a real-world concept (APR comparison, amortization schedule)

Module Discussion Guides

Use these questions during or after each module. No prior teacher expertise in finance required — the questions are designed to surface student reasoning, not test recall.

Module 0 — Budget Baseline (Onboarding)

  • You chose [scenario]. What surprised you about the income and expenses in that starting situation?
  • Jordan earns $800/month but Alex earns $3,500. What life decisions might explain that difference?
  • What does "cash flow" mean in the game? How is it different from "salary"?
  • If your cash flow was negative in Month 1, what would you change first — income or spending? Why?

Module 1 — Credit Card Debt Simulator

  • Move the payment slider to "minimum only." How many years until the $5,000 balance is paid off? Does that surprise you?
  • What is APR? The game uses 24% — is that realistic? (The U.S. average in 2025 was 24.4% according to the CFPB.)
  • Paying $25 extra per month reduced the payoff by how many years? What does that tell you about small habits?
  • When would it ever make sense to carry a credit card balance? Are there situations where debt can be "worth it"?
  • The game has an emergency fund that events can drain. How does not having savings affect your debt situation?

Module 2 — Debt Repayment Strategies

  • What is the difference between the avalanche method and the snowball method?
  • The avalanche method saves more money in interest. The snowball method is more motivating. Which would you personally use?
  • Why might someone with three different debts feel overwhelmed even if their total debt isn't that large?
  • What role does behavior and psychology play in personal finance decisions?

Module 3A — Index Funds & Investing

  • Why did the index fund in the simulator grow more steadily than the individual stocks?
  • What is a "fee eater"? How does a 1% annual fee affect a $10,000 investment over 30 years?
  • If the market dropped 20% in the simulation, what would you do — sell, hold, or buy more?
  • What is the difference between a stock and an index fund? Which has more risk? Why?

Module 3B — 401(k) & Employer Match

  • The employer match bar shows "free money." Why do some people still don't contribute enough to get the full match?
  • What does it mean for money to be "tax-advantaged"? Why does that matter?
  • If you had to choose between paying off credit card debt and contributing to a 401(k) with a full employer match, which would you do first?

Module 3C — Mortgages

  • Look at the first month of your amortization. What percentage of the payment went to principal? To interest? Does that seem fair?
  • Why does a 30-year mortgage end up costing so much more than a 15-year mortgage for the same house?
  • What is PMI? Why does the bank require it if your down payment is less than 20%?
  • The ARM (adjustable-rate mortgage) starts cheaper. Why might that be risky over 30 years?
  • Is buying always better than renting? What factors would change your answer?

Life Event Cards (General)

  • Which life event card was the hardest decision to make? Why?
  • When you got a "job loss" card, what did you do first — cut expenses, use savings, or use credit?
  • The game gives you choices but no "right answer." How is that similar to real financial decisions?
  • What would you do differently in real life based on what happened in the game?
  • Why does the game not show a grade or score on the Month Summary screen?

Month Summary & Net Worth

  • After 12 months, is your net worth higher or lower than when you started? What drove the biggest change?
  • What is the difference between "income," "cash flow," and "net worth"?
  • The game uses net worth as the primary score in later levels. Is that a good measure of financial health? What might it miss?
  • What one decision, made differently, would have changed your outcome the most?

State Personal Finance Graduation Mandates

As of 2026, 30 U.S. states require a personal finance course for high school graduation. FinanceGame content is designed to support these mandates across all 30 states. Consult your state's department of education for specific curriculum alignment requirements.

Suggested Assessment Approaches

  • Reflection journal — After each session, students write 2–3 sentences: "What happened? What did I decide? What would I change?"
  • Exit ticket — One real-world connection: "Name one financial decision you or your family made recently that relates to what you played today."
  • Month 6 compare — At game month 6, students compare net worth with a partner. Discuss: why are the numbers different if you started the same?
  • Avoid grading game performance — Grading in-game outcomes penalizes students who took educational risks (e.g., trying a bad strategy to see what happens). Grade reflection quality instead.

Pedagogical Foundation

This game uses just-in-time instruction — financial concepts appear at the moment of decision, not in advance. Research by Kaiser & Menkhoff (World Bank, 2017) found this approach produces 48% larger learning effects than traditional advance instruction across 126 studies. The life event card mechanic is specifically designed around this principle.
